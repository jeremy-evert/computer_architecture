# Professor Podman

**Status:** Canonical working persona for *Podman at the Podium*  
**Incubator:** COMSC-3013 Computer Architecture  
**Established:** 2026-08-27

## The role

*Podman at the Podium* has a three-professor model:

1. **Jeremy — the builder.** Brings the machine question, does the homework, prepares the experiment, breaks things, measures things, and is willing to be wrong in public.
2. **Professor Podman — the interrogator.** Protects the experimental contract. Asks what changed, what stayed fixed, what was actually measured, and whether the evidence supports the story.
3. **The guest professor — another way of seeing.** Brings a discipline, craft, life experience, or professional lens that makes the machine question richer.

Professor Podman is not an oracle and not a synthetic replacement for a human instructor. Professor Podman is a recurring teaching role: curiosity plus verification, standing beside the Experimental Chamber.

> **The machine is powerful. You are the multiplier.**

Professor Podman's job is to keep asking whether we are actually multiplying anything.

## Warren Akers as the human model

Warren Akers, a mathematics instructor at SWOSU, is the first and preferred human model for Professor Podman.

The point is **not** to turn Warren into a costume or caricature. The point is to learn from qualities Jeremy values in conversations with him: patient curiosity, delight in an interesting question, mathematical instinct, comfort with not knowing yet, and a willingness to follow an idea because it became interesting.

Personal texture such as tea, Birkenstocks, humor, and Warren's relaxed presence may give the role warmth if Warren enjoys that framing. They are seasoning, not the character.

If Warren participates, the role should remain transparent:

> "I'm Warren Akers, and today I'm sitting in as Professor Podman."

Warren should always have agency over how his own personality, stories, voice, and recurring bits are represented.

## Before we build the persona, learn the person

The first conversations with Warren should **not** be architecture interviews and should not be attempts to harvest catchphrases.

### Conversation 1 — Warren

Focus on the human being.

Possible territory:

- Where did he come from?
- Why mathematics?
- What is he working on or thinking about now?
- What gives him energy outside the classroom?
- What makes a problem beautiful or worth pursuing?
- What does he notice that other people often miss?
- What has changed in the way he thinks over his career?

The goal is relationship and understanding, not usable footage.

### Conversation 2 — Curiosity and knowing

Explore how Warren thinks.

Possible questions:

- What do you do when you do not understand something?
- What makes you suspicious of an answer?
- What is the difference between an answer and an explanation?
- When does mathematics tell us something surprising about the physical world?
- How do you know when a model is useful even though it is incomplete?
- What question do you ask when everyone else thinks the problem is finished?
- What do students misunderstand about being "good at math"?

Again, the goal is not to write Professor Podman on the spot. The goal is to discover the real habits of curiosity worth preserving.

## Jeremy's homework rule

A guest should not arrive merely so Jeremy can discover whether there is anything interesting to talk about.

Before a technical recording, Jeremy should already have:

- a real question;
- a bounded demonstration, artifact, dataset, machine, graph, trace, or result;
- enough background research to explain why it matters;
- an initial hypothesis or interpretation;
- receipts showing that the interesting phenomenon is real and reasonably reproducible;
- at least one place where the result remains open to interpretation.

Then the guest gets to move the conversation wherever genuine curiosity takes it.

> **Do the discovery needed to find a good question before the guest arrives. Do not pre-discover the guest's answer.**

## The "you need to come see this" threshold

Do not manufacture a guest episode because the calendar says one is due.

A strong invitation begins when a demonstration produces the reaction:

> **You need to come see this.**

The machine should earn the guest.

That means the solo Computer Architecture recordings are also the show's test bench. Jeremy and Podman can grind through experiments, tooling, capture, editing, transcripts, recap, and publishing mechanics until something genuinely interesting emerges.

When the experiment makes Jeremy lean toward the screen, it becomes a candidate for a guest.

## Professor Podman's questions

Professor Podman needs a recognizable questioning grammar rather than scripted dialogue.

Useful recurring questions:

- What exactly changed?
- What did we hold constant?
- How do we know?
- What are we actually measuring?
- Is that host-visible or container-visible?
- Is this native, virtualized, containerized, or emulated?
- Did we give the machine more resources, or did we give the **program** a way to use them?
- What assumption are we making?
- What would falsify that explanation?
- Can we reproduce it?
- What happens if we change one thing?
- Why should that curve look like that?
- Where does the model stop matching the machine?
- What did we learn because we were wrong?

Professor Podman should be perfectly comfortable saying:

> **I don't know. How could we find out?**

## Mistakes are continuity, not bloopers

Jeremy will make mistakes. Guests will make predictions that fail. Measurements will be weird. A tool will occasionally lie by omission. That material should not automatically be edited away.

The contract is that a mistake must create a next move.

### Professor's Homework

When a guest exposes a real gap and the answer cannot be established responsibly during the recording, create a **Professor's Homework** thread.

Close the segment with the unanswered question.

Then Jeremy goes away, does the work, preserves the receipts, and returns later with:

1. what we thought;
2. what we did not know;
3. what was tested;
4. what the evidence showed;
5. what explanation changed.

This turns uncertainty into narrative continuity and models a professional response to being wrong.

## Professor Podman is a role, not one required person

Warren may become the canonical recurring human Professor Podman, but the show must not depend on one person's availability.

The role can also be:

- Jeremy explicitly asking the Professor Podman questions himself;
- a visual or audio segment that presents the experimental contract;
- a guest temporarily taking the questioning chair;
- another recurring collaborator who understands the role and respects the evidence contract.

If another person occupies the chair, the bit may be playful, but the function remains the same: **interrogate the experiment, not perform certainty.**

## Cross-disciplinary superpower

Professor Podman allows non-computing guests to matter without forcing them to pretend to be computer architects.

A mathematician can ask about scaling and limits.

A storyteller can ask who the suspects are in a performance mystery.

A kinesiology scholar can test analogies between computer systems and bodies.

An education scholar can compare human learning and machine learning while exposing where the analogy breaks.

A literature scholar can interrogate computing's borrowed metaphors: memory, threads, language, instruction, learning, architecture.

A networking or cyberinfrastructure expert can follow the story once the machine stops being one box and becomes a distributed system.

The guest's job is not to teach Jeremy's syllabus. Their job is to bring a way of seeing that helps everyone notice more.

## Story genres are allowed

Architecture is full of plot.

Episodes may naturally behave like:

- **murder mysteries:** Who killed the speedup?
- **comedies:** We bought absurd hardware and the bottleneck is a CSV parser.
- **tragedies:** Sixteen workers spend their lives waiting for one lock.
- **romances:** Software finally meets an architecture built for its workload.
- **adventures:** Follow one instruction from source code toward physical execution.
- **horror:** Look at the cache misses.

Genre is a teaching lens, not a requirement. Never distort evidence to force a story shape.

## Production principle

Professor Podman should make expertise look more human, not more theatrical.

The desired student experience is not:

> "These professors know everything."

It is:

> **"These people know how to find out."**
