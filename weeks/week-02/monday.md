# Monday - AI can propose; evidence decides

An AI assistant can be useful before you know the answer. It can help you name possibilities, find vocabulary, suggest commands, and turn a vague question into something testable.

That usefulness creates a trap: a plausible explanation can feel like evidence.

For this course, keep four things separate:

1. **Question:** what exactly are you trying to know?
2. **Proposal:** what does a person, AI tool, search result, or prior belief suggest?
3. **Evidence:** what did the machine, source, specification, or measurement actually show?
4. **Conclusion:** what can you now defend, including limits?

## A bounded example

Bad question:

> What kind of computer is this?

Better questions:

- What operating-system family is visible to this process?
- What machine/ISA label does Python report?
- How many logical CPUs are visible to this process?
- What Python version is actually executing my commands?

These can be checked without trusting a model's memory.

## Your prediction

Choose one bounded machine claim you can test Wednesday. Before running the evidence command, write:

- the claim;
- what you currently expect;
- one piece of context that matters;
- what evidence would change your mind.

If you use AI, record the tool and the substance of its proposal. Do not paste a giant chat transcript.

If you do not want to create or use an AI account, use this fallback proposal:

> **Fallback proposal:** "The machine architecture reported by Python and the operating system will always describe the physical processor in exactly the same way."

Your job Wednesday is not to agree with it. Your job is to test what the available evidence actually supports.

## The habit we are building

A good investigator can say:

> "The tool suggested X. I checked Y. The evidence supports X only under Z limitation."

That sentence will age much better than "the chatbot said so."
